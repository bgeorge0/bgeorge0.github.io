Think like a treatment planner

An optimiser is like an automatic planner. You tell it what you want, it tries different beam settings, and it looks for a plan that best balances those goals. In these exercises, you can see the dose change on an axial slice as you change the instructions.

Optional: the maths behind this

For each target pixel, add the square of the dose shortfall, if any. For each rectum pixel, add the square of the dose excess, if any. Average each set of penalties separately and add them.

PTV: mean[max(0, goal − dose)²]
Rectum: mean[max(0, dose − goal)²]
Total = PTV penalty + rectum penalty

A 10 Gy violation has four times the penalty of a 5 Gy violation. In this toy model, dose is proportional to beam output.

Optional: the maths behind this

Each pixel receives dose from several beam components. The computer adds their contributions, then estimates how changing each intensity changes the score. It takes a step in the direction that reduces the score, keeping all beam intensities non-negative.

Dose = sum of (beam intensity × dose contribution)
Total penalty = 8 × target underdose + 0.8 × target overdose + priority × rectum + 0.7 × bladder + small normal-tissue penalty
Next intensity = max(0, current intensity − step × slope)

This is projected gradient descent with backtracking. Changing a priority changes the score itself, so do not compare the total penalty across different priorities as if they were the same quantity.

All else equal, increasing a soft penalty weight makes violating that objective more expensive. The optimiser can still choose to violate it when competing goals cannot all be met. The mathematical optimum is not a clinical approval decision.

01 / The objective function

How bad is a dose?

An optimiser cannot directly interpret “cover the PTV and spare the bowel”. We translate goals into mathematical penalties. A larger penalty means a less desirable result for that particular objective. The optimiser tries to reduce the total penalty, not simply meet every displayed goal.

Violation magnitude
Weighted penalty

The penalty is in illustrative cost units (weight × Gy²), not a probability of harm. Zero cost on one objective does not imply a safe plan.

Try it: With minimum dose 40 Gy, compare actual doses of 35 and 30 Gy. Doubling a violation quadruples its quadratic penalty. Switch to maximum dose: the penalised side reverses.
02 / How the optimiser moves

Gradient descent: walk downhill

Imagine a bowl-shaped landscape. Your position x is the adjustable variable (for instance a beam intensity); the height is cost. The derivative is the slope. Moving opposite the slope usually reduces cost if the step is sufficiently small.

F(x) = (x − 4)²   ·   F′(x) = 2(x − 4)
xnext = x − α F′(x)

Try it: Start at zero, run with α = 0.1, then 0.5, then 1.1. A small step is slow; 0.5 reaches the minimum in one step for this specific quadratic; an excessively large step diverges. Reset between trials.
What about other algorithms?

Steepest descent uses the negative gradient. Conjugate gradient combines current and previous search directions to reduce inefficient zig-zagging. Newton and quasi-Newton methods use curvature (or estimates of it) to scale and direct updates. Line search adapts step size; projection enforces simple constraints such as non-negative fluence. These approaches differ in efficiency and constraints, not in the clinical meaning of the objective. Commercial optimisers vary and may directly optimise deliverable apertures, use additional approximations, or employ proprietary methods. This page demonstrates basic and projected gradient descent, not a specific vendor algorithm.

03 / Competing objectives

Weighting is a trade-off, not a guarantee

Suppose a single beam delivers dose x to a target and 0.8x to a nearby organ. We want 50 Gy to the target and no more than 20 Gy to the organ. In this toy geometry both goals cannot be satisfied simultaneously. Which compromise does the objective favour?

F(x) = max(0, 50 − x)² + w × max(0, 0.8x − 20)²

The target weight is fixed at 1. In this example, the analytic optimum is x* = (50 + 16w)/(1 + 0.64w), choosing x = 50 when w = 0.

Under the hood: dose matrix, objective and algorithm

Each row of A describes an equal-volume voxel; each column represents a synthetic beam component. The coefficients describe dose per unit intensity. The model has 80 voxels and three components. The PTV uses a minimum-dose penalty at 50 Gy (weight 1) and maximum-dose penalty at 53 Gy (weight 0.6). The OAR uses a one-sided maximum-dose penalty at the selected dose and weight. Other tissue uses a dose-squared penalty at weight 0.025. Each term is averaged across its own structure; weighting and normalisation vary across real systems. The gradient is calculated analytically and projected gradient descent with backtracking accepts non-increasing cost steps. Real planning includes 3D anatomy, machine constraints, delivery complexity, dose calculation accuracy, uncertainty and independent clinical review. The toy model has none of these.

D95 uses the dose of the lowest-ranked voxel still covering at least 95% of the PTV by volume (discrete nearest-rank convention). V50 is the percentage receiving ≥50 Gy. OAR maximum is a single-voxel maximum, not a near-maximum volume metric.

11 / IPEM tutorial extension

The surface build-up trap

In photon planning, a superficial region may receive less dose per unit fluence because of build-up. A naive uniform-dose objective can compensate by increasing local fluence. This independent two-voxel example deliberately leaves out scatter, geometry, movement and real depth-dose physics.

F(f) = w × (kf − 50)² + λf²
f* = 50wk/(wk² + λ)
